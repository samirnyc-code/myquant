The short version

Leg count is a beautifully stable feature of the market — and, it turns out, a volatility gauge, not a direction gauge. I reproduced Tim's work exactly, then tested a dozen ways to trade it. Most directional ideas came up empty; the honest, repeatable signal in legs is how much a market moves, not which way.

Validation Confirmed

The pipeline agrees with Tim

Before trusting anything, I checked my engine against Tim's own numbers. Counting legs his way — track the running extreme from the open, close a leg when price reverses by more than 0.15×ADR — my results land on his published figures within 1.2% every year.

That match means the data (16 years of Databento ES 5-minute RTH bars) and the counting logic are sound, so every result that follows rests on solid ground.

Structure Confirmed

Leg count is a stable constant

ES prints about 15 legs a day, and it has for sixteen straight years — every single year sits in a narrow 14.7–16.6 band with no drift. Tim showed this for four years; it holds for a decade before his window too.

The reason it stays flat is the ADR normalization: as volatility rose over the years, the threshold grew with it, so the count stayed put. (The size in ticks is a very different story — see the last chart.)

Prediction Null on trend

Early legs forecast volatility, not trend

The tempting idea — a quiet, few-legged open means a trend day — is false. Early leg count has essentially zero relationship with how directional the day turns out (correlation −0.03). The lone exception is a dead-quiet, one-legged morning (3% of days), which does trend 67% of the time.

What early legs do predict is the rest of the day's range (correlation +0.40, and it holds even after controlling for the morning's range). A busy morning means a busy afternoon. This is the first hint of the theme that runs through everything: legs measure volatility, not direction.

Geometry Finding

Legs hold their size but change speed

A leg is remarkably consistent in size (~0.32×ADR) from the open to the close. What changes is speed: a leg takes ~2.5 bars near the open and close but ~6.7 bars at lunch — the classic midday lull, measured in leg duration.

The sharpest pattern is size mean-reversion: after an unusually big leg, the next one is larger only 14% of the time; after a small leg, 81%. Some of that is regression to the mean, but the magnitude makes it useful for sizing targets.

Day types Null on persistence

A working taxonomy — that doesn't repeat

I built the day-type classifier Tim never finished (Trend-Up 24%, Channel 28%,Range 31%, Trend-Down 16%) from directional structure, and it validates his instinct — but only halfway. Fewer legs does mean more trend for up days (12.6 legs); trend-down days are the choppiest of all (19.2 legs). Selloffs trend and whip at the same time.

The catch for traders: today's type tells you almost nothing about tomorrow's — the transition matrix is flat. Day types don't cluster. Volatility, on the other hand, clearly does: a big-range day is followed by another 58.5% of the time.

Direction Signal

Structure carries a real signal

Finally, something directional. When a leg breaks structure (a new session high/low), the next same-direction leg breaks too 48% of the time; after a leg that stays inside, only 14%. And balance does not coil into a breakout — the more inside legs stack up, the less likely the next one breaks out. Trends persist; ranges persist.

This is the one thing leg count, size, and day-typing all missed — a genuine regime-persistence signal. But before celebrating, I stress-tested it.

Skepticism Partial

…but most of it is mechanical

After a breakout you're sitting right next to the extreme, so making a new one is cheap; deep in a range you're far from it. Once I hold that distance fixed, most of the 48%-vs-14% gap disappears — it was ~85% mechanical positioning.

A small, honest edge survives: +5.4 points of extra breakout probability at equal distance, consistent across every bucket. Real, but modest — the kind of thing that earns a costed backtest, not a victory lap.

Refutation Refuted

“Big days continue” didn't hold up

Tim found that big ES days (range ≥3.3×ABR) get a same-direction second leg 85% of the time — from 14 days over 2021–2025. On my full 16 years, it's the opposite: the bigger the day, the less the next day continues (33% at 3.3×, below the 49% base rate), and the average next-day drift runs against the move.

ES mean-reverts after big days; big up days reverse hardest. His 85% was a small, recent bull-market sample. (Caveat: I tested next-day close/extreme, not his exact intraday pullback-entry rule — but the continuation premise clearly fails.)

Practical Use this

The “scalp” has grown 5×

Here's the finding with the most direct trading consequence. The leg count is constant, but a leg's size in ticks has roughly quintupled — from ~8 ticks (2010–2017) to ~43 ticks (2022, 2025, 2026) — as ES climbed from ~1,300 to ~7,700 and volatility rose.

So a fixed-tick scalp target is completely unmoored from the market: an 8-tick target was a whole leg in 2012 and is under a fifth of one now. This is the strongest case in the whole study for volatility-scaled (ADR-based) targets instead of fixed ticks.

The throughline: leg-based metrics forecast volatility — it clusters, it persists, and leg sizes mean-revert — but they say almost nothing about direction. Build volatility timing and position sizing on legs, not trend prediction.

What's worth doing next

  • Volatility-scaled targets. The scalp has grown 5× — test ADR-based exits against fixed-tick targets on a live scalper.
  • First breakout of the day. The last untested directional idea: does the day's first structure break predict its close?
  • Tag the trade book by leg index. Do entries early in the day beat late, exhausted ones?
  • Cost the breakout edge. Turn the surviving +5.4-point structure edge into a pass/fail after real fills.
Method — a leg opens at the RTH open, tracks the running extreme, and closes when price reverses by more than 0.15×ADR (average of the prior 8 daily ranges), measured on intrabar highs/lows; the count resets each morning.

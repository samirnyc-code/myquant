# Ch 9 — The Seasonality of Markets and the Best Times to Trade

## Summary (3-5 lines)
Halsey argues there are safe and dangerous times to trade at the year, month, week, and hour scale, and that trading the right season/time raises win expectation. The year splits into two seasons — "risk on" and "protect profits" — driven by the fact that most funds are long-only. He maps a money manager's calendar (buy in late Aug/Sep, take profit late Apr/early May, hedge and vacation over summer), identifies Mon/Fri as the worst weekdays and Tue–Thu as the best, and lays out an Eastern-Time clock of the best and worst hours. All times in the chapter are quoted in Eastern Time.

## Key concepts & definitions
- **Two seasons of the year:** "risk on" and "protect profits" (a.k.a. "risk off"). Rooted in the fact that "most funds in the market are long-only funds" — they don't short.
- **Fast / slow times of the year** tie to the best months to trade (Figure 9.1).
- **Participation:** "when traders are actively accumulating positions." Higher participation = more technical, faster-trading market (occurs mid-week).
- **Daily doldrums:** the slowest recurring parts of the 24-hour cycle (Asian session; Europe's lunch; the US lunch after 11:30).
- **Match the speed of the market:** fast markets should be traded quickly (more trades); slow markets traded slowly (fewer trades). New traders wrongly equate slow=safe, fast=danger — "exactly the opposite is true."

## Rules / setups / mechanics

### Months (best/worst to buy and sell)
- "Sell in May and go away" and the "Santa Claus rally" are treated as real patterns.
- Best time to be bullish: **from Labor Day through Memorial Day.**
- **April = best month to sell.**
- **May = worst month to buy.**
- **End of August = worst month to sell and the best to be a buyer.**
- Long-term bullish plan: **be a buyer in late August and September; take profits in late April and early May.**
- From **end of May to end of August** = one of the slowest trading/participation periods. "The 90 days of summer" = most dangerous markets (consolidations on daily charts, tight intraday ranges, low-participation breakouts/breakdowns).
- **October to March has the best returns over the past 100 years.**
- Long-term stance: "being a bull from September to March and going on a long summer vacation."

### A Money Manager's Year (seasonality points)
- **Summer:** Coming into Memorial Day, managers are fully invested with new client money from the prior Aug–Sep. They (1) take partial profit and (2) completely hedge market exposure. Hedged = free for summer vacation (the Hamptons); support staff runs daily ops; new client money still coming in → explains low summer volume ("Big Money has prepared itself for a choppy, low-participation summer").
- **Fall:** After Labor Day, volume picks up. First action: remove the summer protection "hedges" (the "risk off" trade) — this unwinding = the end-of-summer volatility spike. Next: put accumulated new client money to work → the "risk on" trade begins; accumulation into winter = "Santa Claus rally." This makes **August the worst month to sell and September the best to buy.**
- **Winter & Spring:** Oct–Mar best returns; continuation of accumulation. Into April/May the market slows, volume dries up, trend participation falters → summer begins again. This makes **April the best month to sell/take profit and May the worst to be a buyer.**
- **Typical money manager schedule (composite):** works ~9 months/year, takes the whole summer off; comes in Monday morning but gets nothing done until Monday afternoon or Tuesday morning; finishes Friday by lunch; the three productive days they are active **10:00 a.m. to 11:30 a.m.**, then a two-hour lunch, back at **1:30 p.m.** for a late-afternoon push into the close **until 4:00 p.m.**

### Days of the week
- **Mondays and Fridays = the worst days of the week to trade.**
- **Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday = highest-volume, "normal" trading days** — where "all the heavy lifting for the week" and most trade setups/target completions happen.
- Monday: slow morning, low volume; **volume usually starts to pick up in the afternoon around 1:30 to 2:00 p.m.** Trade Mondays with half-size positions if at all.
- Friday: **by the European session close at 11:30 a.m. Eastern Time** most volume has dried up; price can nearly stop trading. "More profitable to not trade Friday afternoons."

### Hours — best times to trade (all Eastern Time)
- **2 A.M. ET:** Frankfurt (German) exchange opens; breaks the slow Asian session. Observe direction for the first 15 minutes of the day.
- **3 A.M. ET:** London opens — "real volume comes into the market." Often the exact reversal of the German open. Normal volume from European open to Europe's lunch.
- **5:30 A.M. ET:** Start of Europe's daily doldrums, slowest part of the day. Profit taking in the opposite direction of the 3:00 a.m. direction (Europe's lunchtime reversal, start of consolidation).
- **7:30 A.M. ET:** End of Europe's daily doldrums; frequently a stop run out of the lunchtime consolidation.
- **8:00–8:30 A.M. ET:** NYSE futures open; US volume overlaps Europe. **From 8:00 a.m. to 11:00 a.m. the highest volume of the day is produced** — "the best time to trade futures and foreign exchange markets." An attempt at the gap fill occurs 90 minutes before the opening bell.
- **10 A.M. ET:** The first 30 minutes of the day is a no-trade zone; Big Money waits until after the first half-hour. Creates a reliable **10:00 a.m. volume push** — "produces more entries than any other, and is often the high or the low of any given trading day." Volume/participation from 10:00 a.m. lasts 90 minutes.
- **1:30–2:00 P.M. ET:** End of the daily doldrums; often a stop run of the lunchtime consolidation. Afternoon usually continues the trend that began at 10:00 a.m.
- **3:30–3:45 P.M. ET:** Last volume push of the day; an attempt to get the market back to its average price. Target "calculated by adding the high of the day plus the low of the day, divided by the current price trading."

### Hours — worst times to trade (all Eastern Time)
- **6:30 P.M.–2:00 A.M. ET:** Beginning of the Asian session, the "daily doldrums" of the 24-hour cycle. Two major-volume continents asleep — "best to stay out of markets altogether."
- **5:30 A.M.–7:30 A.M. ET:** Europe's daily doldrums / lunch break. Limit trades to larger time frames or managing positions already on.
- **9:30 A.M.–10:00 A.M. ET:** NYSE opens 9:30; the first half-hour is a no-trade zone — most volatile time, reactionary trades and stop runs. Trades initiated 9:30–10:00 a.m. have very low profit expectation. "Wait for 10:00 a.m."
- **11:30 A.M. ET:** The 11:30 reversal period; European session close; start of US lunch/doldrums. Usually profit taking and reversal from the 10:00 a.m. direction; slowest part of the day.

## Codifiable vs discretionary
- **Codifiable:** month labels/seasons (Fig 9.1); day-of-week volume ranking (Mon/Fri low, Tue–Thu high); all clock windows in ET (2:00, 3:00, 5:30, 7:30, 8:00–8:30, 8:00–11:00 highest-volume block, 9:30–10:00 no-trade, 10:00 push +90 min, 11:30 reversal, 1:30–2:00, 3:30–3:45, 6:30 p.m.–2:00 a.m. Asian doldrums); half-size on Mondays; average-price target formula (H+L)/current price; 15-minute direction read after the 2 a.m. open.
- **Discretionary:** how to weigh "match the speed of the market"; whether to trade at all on Mon/Fri; using seasonality as a directional bias vs. a hard filter; interpreting "risk on / protect profits" for a given year.
